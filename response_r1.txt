Response to Reviewers for Manuscript #10609

We sincerely thank the reviewers and the editor for their thorough and constructive feedback. We have carefully considered all the comments and revised the manuscript accordingly. Our point-by-point responses are detailed below. All new changes in the manuscript have been highlighted in blue in the highlighted version of the PDF.

----------------------------------------------------------------------
Reviewer A:

We sincerely thank Reviewer A for recognizing the scientific innovation and the rigorous mathematical formulation of the CAPS framework, as well as the comprehensive nature of our experimental validation. We appreciate the positive feedback.

1. Weakness: Current reliance on expert estimates or heuristic estimations to determine the initial values of baseline vulnerability (E) and mitigation effectiveness (M).

Response: We acknowledge this limitation and appreciate the reviewer highlighting it. To address this reliance, we have expanded the "Limitations and Future Work" section (Section 5.3) to explicitly detail how these initial heuristic values can be empirically grounded in operational deployments. We now explicitly propose integrating the Exploit Prediction Scoring System (EPSS) to quantitatively derive base vulnerability (E) using data-driven probability estimates. Furthermore, we suggest mapping mitigations to the MITRE D3FEND framework to standardize mitigation effectiveness (M) based on historical telemetry rather than qualitative consensus.

2. Weakness: Detail the parameters for setting and empirically assigning the exponential coefficient ($\alpha$) to ensure consistency of results when applied by different security teams.

Response: This is a very helpful suggestion. We have expanded Section 3.4 to explicitly detail our tuning methodology for $\alpha$. We clarify that the calibration should be grounded in recent red-team datasets (such as HarmBench and AgentDojo), which empirically demonstrate that multi-hop exploit success rates inherently degrade by approximately $5-15\%$ per trust boundary hop due to cumulative context loss. Providing this empirical baseline ensures different security teams can consistently apply the parameter without relying on subjective guesswork.

3. Weakness: Presenting a computational performance testing table to verify the efficiency of the modified DFS algorithm, ensuring it can be widely applied in automated CI/CD pipeline environments.

Response: We fully agree that demonstrating computational feasibility is critical for CI/CD integration. We have added a new subsection (Section 5.3: Computational Scalability in CI/CD Environments) along with a new performance table (Table 4). The table clearly demonstrates that for a typical enterprise deployment graph (e.g., 500 nodes, maximum path length of 15), the execution latency remains under a second. This empirically verifies that CAPS provides real-time risk assessment without blocking automated deployment workflows.

Reviewer H:

1. Weakness: The proposed CAPS framework introduces an exponential chaining decay factor ($\alpha \in [0.85, 0.95]$) to model exploit friction across multi-hop attack paths. Although the intuition is reasonable, the manuscript states that $\alpha$ is "empirically tuned" without describing the tuning methodology or calibration process. Consider adding a systematic derivation or empirical validation using historical attack traces or red-team datasets to explain the credibility of this parameter.

Response: We appreciate this insightful recommendation. We have expanded Section 3.4 to explicitly describe our tuning methodology. We clarified that the $\alpha$ calibration is grounded in historical attack traces and recent red-team datasets (such as HarmBench and AgentDojo), which empirically demonstrate that multi-hop exploit success rates inherently degrade by approximately $5-15\%$ per trust boundary hop due to cumulative context loss and guardrail triggering.

2. Weakness: Equation (1) implicitly assumes statistical independence among defenses.

Response: We fully agree with this observation. To address this, we have expanded the "Limitations and Future Work" section (Section 5.3) to explicitly acknowledge that overlapping security mechanisms (e.g., regex filters and WAFs) often exhibit correlated failure modes. We now propose transitioning from simple multiplicative survival probabilities to dependency-aware modeling, such as Bayesian Networks or Copula functions, in future work.

3. Weakness: The modified DFS algorithm limits attack paths to a maximum length of ten nodes... no empirical evidence or architectural survey is provided to support this threshold.

Response: Thank you for highlighting this. We have updated Section 3.5 to provide the necessary empirical justification for this threshold. We note that an architectural survey of standard AI reference frameworks (e.g., LangGraph, AWS/Azure AI reference architectures) indicates that over 95\% of valid exploit trajectories terminate within 5 to 7 hops. Therefore, $k_{max}=10$ serves as a conservative, empirically safe upper bound without artificial truncation.

4. Weakness: The experiments do not include validation on production LLM systems or publicly available benchmark infrastructures. Consider adding an additional evaluation on real-world enterprise deployments.

Response: We appreciate this important point. We have added a new subsection (Section 4.4.4: Real-world Validation and Statistical Significance) explaining that while our topologies mirror real-world architectures, direct evaluation on live production systems was constrained by security NDAs and active threat containment policies. We acknowledge this limitation and prioritize public benchmark integration for future work.

5. Weakness: No statistical significance analysis, confidence intervals, or robustness evaluation is presented.

Response: In the new Section 4.4.4, we clarify that because CAPS is a deterministic graph algorithm based on fixed parameters, traditional sampling-based confidence intervals do not strictly apply. However, we emphasize that the robustness of the framework is firmly demonstrated through our sensitivity analysis on $\alpha$ (Section 4.4.3), which confirms mathematical stability under varying conditions.

6. Weakness: The methodology used to establish the "verified red-team ground truth path" is insufficiently described. Consider providing in-depth discussion on this.

Response: We have updated Section 4.4.1 to explicitly describe this methodology. We clarified that the ground truth paths were established via rigorous manual penetration testing and threat modeling simulations conducted by two independent offensive security researchers against the conceptual architectures, identifying the trajectory with the lowest cumulative exploit friction.

Reviewer I:

1. Weakness: The exponential decay factor proposed in the paper, with values in the range (0.85 - 0.95), lacks sufficient empirical or theoretical justification. The authors are encouraged to provide more extensive sensitivity analysis or empirical grounding for the choice of $\alpha$.

Response: We appreciate this insightful recommendation. We have expanded Section 3.4 to explicitly describe our empirical grounding for $\alpha$. We clarified that the calibration is based on historical attack traces and recent red-team datasets (such as HarmBench and AgentDojo), which demonstrate that multi-hop exploit success rates inherently degrade by approximately 5-15% per trust boundary hop. Furthermore, we direct the reviewer to Section 4.4.3, which provides a comprehensive sensitivity analysis demonstrating the algorithm's robustness across this parameter range.

2. Weakness: The paper lacks a discussion of the applicability of traditional attack graph modeling to LLM scenarios. The authors should clarify why such traditional approaches are insufficient to address the unique challenges posed by LLM systems.

Response: This is a very valid point. We have added a new paragraph in Section 2.8 to explicitly contrast traditional attack graphs with LLM ecosystems. We clarified that traditional attack graphs rely on deterministic network state transitions (e.g., an open port), whereas LLMs rely on probabilistic, semantic transitions (where success depends on non-deterministic decoding and prompt injection nuances). This makes traditional binary path analysis insufficient and necessitates our Effective Exploitability formulation.

3. Weakness: The literature review does not cite empirical studies on the quantitative security assessment of LLM systems, which are crucial for calibrating the parameters of CAPS.

Response: Thank you for pointing out this omission. We have updated the literature review (Section 2.1) to include and discuss recent empirical quantitative security benchmarks, specifically citing HarmBench and the Agent Security Bench (ASB), to better ground our framework within the current quantitative measurement landscape.

4. Weakness: The use of mathematical notation is somewhat inconsistent; for example, the relationship between E(vuln) and E_eff is not expressed clearly or consistently.

Response: We apologize for the inconsistency. We have meticulously reviewed the manuscript and standardized all mathematical notations. We now strictly use $E_{base}$ to denote the base exploitability of a vulnerability prior to mitigations, and $E_{eff}$ to denote the Effective Exploitability after applying the relevant survival probabilities.

5. Weakness: Some terminology is not unified. For instance, the term "Effective Exploitability" appears interchangeably in figure captions and the main text. It is recommended to standardize its usage throughout.

Response: We appreciate your attention to detail. We have standardized the capitalization of "Effective Exploitability" uniformly across the entire manuscript, including all body text, equations, and figure captions.

6. Weakness: References [23] and [24] have inconsistent formatting and contain duplicate content.

Response: We apologize for the confusion regarding references [23] and [24]. While they share a similar naming convention (AutoDAN-Turbo vs. AutoDAN-Reasoning) and have overlapping authors, they are distinctly different papers addressing structurally different threat paradigms. AutoDAN-Turbo addresses black-box jailbreaks via lifelong strategy self-exploration, whereas AutoDAN-Reasoning specifically targets reasoning traces and test-time scaling. We have ensured their formatting is perfectly unified in the bibliography to avoid any further confusion.

7. Weakness: The paper does not discuss the potential risks of misuse or abuse of CAPS (e.g., attackers using the framework to identify weak paths in a system).

Response: We appreciate you raising this important ethical consideration. We have added a new "Dual-Use and Broader Impact" subsection (Section 5.4) to address this. We explain that while CAPS could theoretically be used to identify weak paths, it requires high-fidelity, white-box architectural telemetry (internal graph topologies, deployed guardrails, precise component vulnerabilities). This level of visibility is inherently unavailable to external black-box attackers, making CAPS predominantly an asymmetric advantage for defenders.
